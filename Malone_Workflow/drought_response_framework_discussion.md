# A Framework for Interpreting Ecosystem Drought Responses Through Transpiration-Based Water Use Efficiency

## Overview

Drought does not impair all ecosystems equally, nor does it always impair them at all. A central challenge in ecosystem ecology is distinguishing ecosystems that adjust to water limitation — tightening the coupling between carbon gain and water loss — from those that are genuinely impaired by it, where physiological capacity degrades and recovery is slow or incomplete. Transpiration-based water use efficiency (WUE_T = GPP / T) offers a precise lens for this distinction because it isolates the stomatal and biochemical processes most directly regulated by water stress, separating them from soil evaporative fluxes that can confound whole-canopy ET signals. When WUE_T is tracked against SPEI drought indices across multiple timescales, two mechanistically distinct response trajectories emerge: **drought adjustment** and **drought impairment**. Together, these define a response framework that is especially informative for understanding the resilience of coastal ecosystems, where salinity gradients, tidal connectivity, and water table proximity create a physiological context fundamentally different from upland vegetation.

---

## Drought Adjustment: Stomatal Regulation and Isohydric Conservation

During moderate to severe drought (negative SPEI), some ecosystems exhibit increases in WUE_T rather than declines. This counterintuitive response reflects stomatal downregulation that reduces transpiration proportionally more than it reduces GPP. When stomata partially close in response to elevated vapor pressure deficit or declining soil moisture, the plant sacrifices some carbon assimilation but sharply curtails water loss; the net effect is a higher ratio of carbon gained per unit water transpired. This is the signature of **drought adjustment** — an active, regulatory response rather than a passive collapse.

In coastal ecosystems, this adjustment pathway is particularly evident in saline and brackish sites, where plants operate under chronic osmotic stress even under non-drought conditions. These communities — including salt marsh grasses (*Spartina*, *Distichlis*), mangroves, and halophytes — have evolved constitutively conservative stomatal behavior and high intrinsic WUE. When meteorological drought compounds existing osmotic constraints, these species may show relatively stable or even elevated WUE_T because their baseline stomatal conductance is already low, leaving less room for further decline in transpiration but also less vulnerability to runaway water loss. Empirically, this manifests as smaller WUE_T anomalies during dry SPEI periods compared to upland or freshwater sites — not because saline ecosystems are unaffected by drought, but because they are already operating near the isohydric end of the stomatal regulation spectrum. The transpiration ratio (T/ET) is a useful coupling metric here: sites with high T/ET are more transpiration-dominated and show stronger WUE_T sensitivity to SPEI, while sites where evaporation dominates (shallow open water, sparse canopy) show weaker WUE_T drought signals because the transpiration pathway is less prominent to begin with.

A key implication of the adjustment pathway is that GPP and T decline together but WUE_T rises, meaning the ecosystem maintains carbon capture efficiency even as absolute flux magnitudes fall. Recovery after drought is typically rapid in adjustment-dominated ecosystems: once soil moisture or SPEI rebounds, stomatal conductance recovers, and WUE_T returns toward its near-normal baseline without a persistent legacy effect. This pattern characterizes resilient ecosystems — those whose structure and function are not permanently altered by the drought event.

## Drought Impairment: Physiological Damage and Legacy Effects

The second trajectory — **drought impairment** — represents a qualitatively different ecosystem state. Here, severe or prolonged drought (extreme negative SPEI, particularly at longer accumulation windows such as SPEI-12 to SPEI-48) triggers physiological damage that reduces both GPP and T, but with disproportionate suppression of GPP relative to water loss. The result is a decline in WUE_T during or following the drought event. Mechanistically, this can arise through several pathways: hydraulic failure and xylem cavitation, which interrupt water transport even after atmospheric demand eases; photoinhibition and enzyme denaturation in severely desiccated canopies; sustained reductions in leaf area index that persist beyond the drought itself; and, in coastal systems, salt accumulation in the root zone that converts drought-induced osmotic stress into lasting ion toxicity.

Drought impairment is identifiable not just by the sign of WUE_T anomaly during drought but by the trajectory of recovery. In impaired ecosystems, WUE_T does not rebound promptly with improving SPEI; instead, there is a persistent post-drought depression that can extend months to years. This legacy signal — a WUE_T deficit during near-normal or even wet conditions following drought — suggests that the ecosystem's carbon assimilation capacity has been structurally degraded: canopy replaced by bare substrate, root mortality reducing water uptake capacity, or community composition shifts toward drought-tolerant but less productive species. Reduced transpiration in this context is not a regulatory response but a symptom of canopy loss, and the decoupling of T from its pre-drought baseline is an early indicator of trajectory change.

In coastal ecosystems, impairment is modulated by proximity to the ocean and the nature of the hydrological connection. Freshwater coastal wetlands and upland forests near the coast lack the osmotic buffering of saline communities and are therefore more vulnerable to severe meteorological drought: without marine connectivity to maintain soil moisture, these systems experience both the atmospheric demand of drought and full soil water depletion. The Gulf Coast and Southeast Atlantic regions are particularly relevant here, where freshwater tidal wetlands intermix with brackish marshes across narrow salinity gradients that can shift substantially during drought-driven reductions in freshwater discharge. When drought is severe enough to push brackish sites toward saline conditions — or to allow saltwater intrusion inland — impairment can be compounded by salinity stress layered on top of water deficit, producing WUE_T signals that are difficult to distinguish from direct drought effects without explicit salinity metadata.

## Linking the Two Outcomes: Resilience as a Continuum

The drought adjustment and impairment pathways are not entirely discrete; they define the endpoints of a resilience continuum along which any given site or region may lie depending on drought severity, duration, ecosystem type, and antecedent conditions. SPEI timescale matters critically in this framing: short-accumulation SPEI (1–3 months) captures acute, seasonally transient moisture deficits to which most vegetation can adjust; long-accumulation SPEI (24–48 months) reflects prolonged multi-year droughts that progressively exhaust carbohydrate reserves, deplete soil water storage, and cross physiological thresholds from which recovery is slow. The spatial SPEI-3 pipeline developed in this project — masking global monthly drought indices to the US coastal buffer — positions the analysis to detect precisely these distinctions across the five coastal regions, from the Pacific coast's Mediterranean-climate drought seasonality to the Gulf's hurricane-linked wet-dry cycles.

What makes coastal resilience conceptually distinct is the presence of alternative water sources and hydraulic subsidies that can buffer or mask drought responses. Tidal flooding, groundwater connectivity, and fog interception in Pacific coastal forests can sustain transpiration during periods when meteorological drought would otherwise drive WUE_T upward through stomatal closure. This means that some coastal ecosystems may show muted or absent WUE_T drought responses not because they are impaired but because they are hydraulically subsidized — a form of resilience that is functionally different from isohydric adjustment but equally important for coastal carbon storage. Distinguishing hydraulic buffering from true insensitivity requires the combination of WUE_T with the transpiration ratio and spatial SPEI fields: sites near the coast that maintain high T/ET during dry SPEI periods are likely drawing on alternative water sources, while inland sites showing the same pattern may simply have deep roots accessing groundwater.

Together, the adjustment–impairment framework and its spatial application across coastal SPEI fields provide a mechanistic structure for predicting where and when coastal ecosystems transition from resilient to vulnerable states — a distinction with direct implications for carbon accounting, blue carbon conservation, and the management of coastal wetlands under a drying climate.
